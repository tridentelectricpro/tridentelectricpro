Electrician for Power Outage: What to Check and When to Call

A power outage can mean a tripped main breaker, a failed panel, or a utility problem. Here is how to tell the difference, what to check safely, and when to call an electrician.

Losing power at home is stressful, especially when you do not know whether the problem is inside your house or out on the street. Before you call anyone, a few quick checks can tell you a lot about what is going on and who is responsible for fixing it. Some outages belong to your utility company, and no electrician can restore power until the utility repairs its side of the system. Others start inside your own electrical panel, and those are exactly the kind of problem a licensed electrician can solve. This guide walks you through the checks in order, explains what an electrician does on a power-outage call, and covers backup-power options for the next outage. We serve homeowners in Bellevue and across the greater Seattle area, and our emergency line is staffed around the clock for urgent electrical problems. If anything looks damaged, smells like burning, or makes you unsure, stop and call a licensed electrician rather than trying to fix it yourself.

First Checks: Neighbours, the Utility Map, and Your Breakers

Start by looking outside. If your neighbours' lights are out too, or the streetlights are dark, the outage is almost certainly a utility problem affecting your area. Check your utility's outage map or outage line next. Most utilities in the Seattle area publish a live map showing reported outages, estimated restoration times, and the number of customers affected. If your home is listed, report the outage if it is not already shown, then wait for the utility to restore service. There is nothing an electrician can do while the utility's supply is down. If your neighbours still have power, the problem is more likely inside your home, so head to your electrical panel. Look for the main breaker first. If it has tripped to the off or middle position, that tells you something overloaded or faulted. Before resetting it, turn off or unplug large appliances such as air conditioners, space heaters, and dryers, so the circuit does not trip again the moment you restore power. Push the main breaker firmly to off, then back to on. If power returns and stays on, keep an eye on it and note what was running when it tripped. Also check GFCI and AFCI breakers or outlets. A tripped GFCI outlet in a kitchen or bathroom can cut power to several outlets downstream, and an AFCI breaker can trip when it detects arcing. Reset a GFCI by pressing the reset button firmly. If it trips again immediately, leave it off and call an electrician. Never hold a breaker in the on position, tape it, or replace it with a larger one. If the panel feels hot, smells like burning, or shows scorch marks, do not touch it. Step away and call our 24/7 emergency line.

Is It the Utility's Problem or Your Panel?

Telling the difference between a utility outage and a problem in your own wiring matters, because it decides who to call and how fast the power can come back. A utility problem usually affects more than one home. Whole streets or blocks go dark at once, and the outage often follows a storm, a fallen tree, a vehicle hitting a pole, or planned maintenance. In that case your utility is responsible for the repair, and an electrician cannot speed up the restoration. Your job is to report the outage, stay clear of any downed lines, and keep your home safe until power returns. A problem in your panel usually affects only your home. Your neighbours have power, the utility map shows no outage at your address, and yet your lights and outlets are dead. Common causes include a tripped or failed main breaker, a loose or damaged service connection, a failed meter base, or damage to the service cable where it enters your house. Partial power is another clue. If half of your home works and the other half does not, or if your 240-volt appliances such as the range or dryer have stopped while lights still work, you may have lost one leg of your electrical service. That is a serious condition. Turn off major appliances and call an electrician promptly, because a lost leg can damage equipment and points to a failing connection. Flickering or dimming that started before the outage is also worth mentioning when you call. It can help the electrician trace the problem faster. When you are unsure which side the problem is on, call the utility first to check for an outage at your address, then call a licensed electrician if the utility confirms its service is normal.

What an Electrician Does on a Power-Outage Call

When we arrive for a power-outage call, our first job is to find out where the power stops. We start at the meter and the service entrance, checking whether voltage is reaching your home from the utility. If the utility supply is present but your panel is dead, the fault is on your side of the meter, and we work inward from there. We inspect the main breaker for signs of failure, test it, and check the bus bars and main lugs inside the panel for heat damage, corrosion, or loose connections. Loose connections are a frequent cause of outages, and they can also be a fire risk, so we tighten and test every connection we touch. We also look at the service cable and the weatherhead where the line enters your home, checking for visible damage, water entry, or worn insulation. If the problem is a single circuit rather than the whole house, we trace that circuit through the panel, checking the individual breaker, the wiring, and the outlets or fixtures it feeds. A breaker that trips the moment it is reset tells us there is a short circuit or a ground fault somewhere on that circuit, and we isolate it section by section until we find it. We test GFCI and AFCI devices as well, since a faulty one can cut power to a whole run of outlets. Once we find the cause, we explain it in plain language before doing any repair, so you know what failed and why. If the panel itself is failing, or the service equipment is damaged, we will tell you what needs replacing and what can wait. After the repair, we restore power circuit by circuit, checking that everything holds and that there are no signs of overheating before we leave.

Generator and Backup-Power Options in General Terms

A backup generator can keep your lights, refrigerator, and heating running during an outage, but the type you choose and how it is connected matter a great deal. Portable generators are the simplest option. They sit outside, run on fuel, and can power a few essential items through extension cords or a manual transfer switch. They must always run outdoors, well away from windows and doors, because the exhaust contains carbon monoxide, which is colourless and odourless and can be deadly indoors. Never run a portable generator in a garage, even with the door open, and never connect it directly to your home wiring without a transfer switch. Backfeeding power into your panel through an outlet is extremely dangerous. It can energise lines that utility workers believe are dead, and it can destroy your generator and your appliances when utility power returns. A manual transfer switch, installed by a licensed electrician, lets you safely switch selected circuits to generator power during an outage. Standby generators are permanently installed outside your home and start automatically when the power goes out. They are connected through an automatic transfer switch, so there is nothing to set up in the dark. Whichever option you consider, the transfer switch is the key safety device. It isolates your home from the utility grid while the generator runs. Battery backup systems are another option for short outages, keeping a few circuits running quietly until the grid returns. Before you buy any backup equipment, talk to a licensed electrician about your panel, your essential loads, and the safest way to connect it. We install generator transfer switches and can help you plan a setup that fits your home.

What to Do During an Extended Outage

When an outage lasts for hours, your first priorities are safety, food, and staying warm or cool. Keep the refrigerator and freezer doors closed as much as possible. A closed refrigerator keeps food cold for several hours, and a full freezer holds its temperature longer than a partly full one. As a general rule, food in the fridge that has been above normal fridge temperature for a long time should be treated with caution, and when in doubt, throw it out rather than risk eating spoiled food. Do not taste food to check whether it is safe. If you are unsure, discard it. Use flashlights rather than candles for light, since candles are a fire risk in a dark house where people are moving around. If you use a space heater, keep it away from curtains, bedding, and furniture, and never leave it running while you sleep. Never use a gas stove, oven, or outdoor grill to heat your home. They produce carbon monoxide, and the risk is not worth it. Unplug sensitive electronics and major appliances during the outage. When power returns, it can surge, and unplugging protects your equipment from damage. Leave one light switched on so you will know when power is restored. Check on elderly neighbours or anyone who relies on powered medical equipment, and help them contact their utility or emergency services if needed. If you rely on a sump pump, a well pump, or powered medical equipment, talk to an electrician before the next outage about backup power, so you have a plan in place. Once power is back, plug appliances in one at a time, and watch for anything that smells, sparks, or trips a breaker, which is a sign to call an electrician.

Some power problems can wait for a weekday appointment, and some cannot. Call our 24/7 emergency line right away if you smell burning, see smoke or scorch marks around your panel, hear buzzing or popping from the panel, or see sparking. Call immediately if the panel is hot to the touch, if you see water entering the panel, or if power is out in only part of your home after a storm. A partial outage can mean a failing service connection, which needs attention before it gets worse. Call us any time of day or night if your home has no power at all and the utility has confirmed there is no outage at your address. FAQ

Electrical repair questions, answered

Should I call an electrician or the utility first during a power outage?

Check whether your neighbours have power and look at your utility's outage map first. If the outage affects your street, call the utility. If only your home is out and the utility shows no outage at your address, call a licensed electrician.

Why is only half of my house without power?

A partial outage often means you have lost one leg of your electrical service, or a main connection has failed. Turn off major appliances and call a licensed electrician promptly, because a failing service connection can damage equipment and needs professional attention.

Is it safe to reset my main breaker after an outage?

It is usually safe to reset a main breaker once, after turning off large appliances. Push it firmly to off, then back to on. If it trips again immediately, feels hot, or smells like burning, leave it off and call a licensed electrician.

Can an electrician restore power during a utility outage?

No. If the utility supply to your home is down, an electrician cannot restore it. Only the utility can repair its lines and equipment. An electrician can help once the utility confirms its service is normal but your home still has no power.

How long will food last in the fridge during an outage?

A closed refrigerator keeps food cold for several hours, and a full freezer holds temperature longer. Keep the doors shut, avoid opening them to check, and discard food from the fridge you are unsure about rather than risking eating spoiled food. A fridge thermometer helps you judge the temperature when the power comes back.

Do I need a transfer switch for a portable generator?

Yes. A transfer switch installed by a licensed electrician is the safe way to connect a generator to your home. It prevents backfeeding, which can energise utility lines and endanger line workers, and it protects your generator and appliances. Your electrician can size the switch for the circuits you want to back up.
