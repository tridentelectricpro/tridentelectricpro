Step by Step

How new home wiring works, from plans to final inspection

Most new-construction electrical follows these seven stages. Exact sequence depends on your local jurisdiction.

StageWhenWhat happens
1. Plan reviewBefore framingReview plans, appliances, heating, EV and smart-home goals; size the service with a load calculation.
2. PermitBefore work startsPrepare permit information and confirm local requirements with the authority having jurisdiction.
3. Service and panelEarly buildCoordinate the utility connection, meter base, grounding, and main panel location.
4. Rough-inAfter framingRun cable, set boxes, and install conduit and low-voltage pathways in framed walls and ceilings.
5. Rough-in inspectionBefore insulationInspector verifies work while it is still visible; corrections happen before drywall.
6. Trim-outAfter drywall and paintInstall devices, fixtures, fans, and panel terminations; label circuits.
7. Test and final inspectionBefore move-inTest circuits, GFCI/AFCI devices, and equipment; complete final inspection and energize.
Load Planning

Sizing the service and panel for the way you will live

A load calculation, not a guess, decides panel size.

What goes into the calculation

Square footage, general lighting and receptacle load, kitchen and laundry appliances, heating and cooling, water heating, EV charging, spas, workshops, and expected future additions are all counted under the electrical code method.

Why it matters

An undersized panel limits what you can add later, while a correctly sized one leaves room for EV charging, a heat pump, solar, or battery storage without a service upgrade. Many new single-family homes are planned around 200-amp service; all-electric or larger homes may need more.

Code & Inspections

Safe, inspected, and documented

New wiring is governed by the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70) as adopted and amended locally.

Do not DIY hazardous electrical work

Do not close walls around unverified electrical work. New-construction wiring should be planned, installed, inspected as required, and tested before finishes conceal it. Panel work, new circuits, high-load equipment, and wet-location wiring should be handled by a qualified electrician.

Protection devices

The code generally requires GFCI protection in wet and damp locations and AFCI protection for most living-area circuits, and recent editions require surge protection at dwelling service equipment. Confirm the edition in force with your local building department. Reference: NFPA 70.

Glossary

New-construction electrical terms

Load calculation
A code-based method for adding up expected demand to size the service and panel.
Rough-in
Wiring and boxes installed in open framing before insulation and drywall.
Trim-out
Installation of devices, fixtures, and panel terminations after finishes.
Service entrance
The utility connection, meter, and main disconnect feeding the home.
GFCI
Ground-fault circuit interrupter that cuts power quickly on a ground fault, protecting against shock.
AFCI
Arc-fault circuit interrupter that detects dangerous arcing in wiring to reduce fire risk.
Dedicated circuit
A circuit serving one appliance or piece of equipment.
FAQ

Frequently asked questions about new home electrical

What is included in new home electrical work?

New home electrical work covers the full system: service and panel installation, circuit planning, rough-in wiring, outlets and switches, lighting and fixture wiring, dedicated appliance and equipment circuits, testing, and coordination with required inspections.

What is electrical rough-in?

Electrical rough-in is the stage where cables, boxes, and conduit are installed inside framed walls, ceilings, and floors before insulation and drywall. It is inspected before walls are closed. Devices, fixtures, and panel covers are installed later during trim-out.

How much does electrical wiring cost for a new home?

Commonly published national estimates for new-construction wiring run roughly $2 to $5 per square foot, but real cost depends on home size, layout, service size, number of circuits, fixtures, EV or smart-home features, and local permit rules. A written estimate based on your plans is the only reliable number.

How long does it take to wire a new house?

Rough-in for an average single-family home commonly takes several working days to a couple of weeks, followed by a shorter trim-out and final testing. Scheduling depends on framing progress, other trades, and inspection availability.

What size electrical panel does a new home need?

Many new single-family homes are planned around a 200-amp service, but the correct size comes from a load calculation that accounts for square footage, appliances, heating and cooling, EV charging, and future additions. Larger homes or all-electric homes may need more.

Can a new home be wired for future EV charging?

Yes. During construction, an electrician can reserve panel capacity and install a dedicated 240-volt circuit or conduit to the garage or driveway, so a Level 2 charger can be added later without opening finished walls.

Do new construction electrical projects need permits and inspections?

In most jurisdictions, yes. New residential wiring typically requires a permit, a rough-in inspection before walls are closed, and a final inspection. Requirements are set locally, so your electrician should confirm them with the local authority having jurisdiction.

Which circuits require GFCI or AFCI protection?

The National Electrical Code, as adopted locally, requires GFCI protection in wet or damp areas such as kitchens, bathrooms, garages, basements, laundry areas, and outdoors, and AFCI protection for most living-area circuits. The edition and amendments in force in your area determine the exact rules.

Can smart-home wiring be installed during new construction?

Yes. Neutral-ready switch boxes, camera and access-point wiring, lighting-control circuits, and low-voltage pathways are much easier and cheaper to install at rough-in than after drywall.

Should I plan for solar, battery storage, or a generator?

If any of these are possible in the next several years, tell your electrician before rough-in. Panel space, main-breaker rating, conduit routes, and a transfer-switch location can all be planned in advance at low cost.

When should I hire an electrician for a new build?

Ideally before framing starts, when plans are still flexible. Early involvement lets the electrician review the layout, size the service, and flag changes that are cheap on paper but expensive once walls are closed.
