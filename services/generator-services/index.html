Direct Answer

What is generator & backup power electrical work?

Generator and backup power electrical work is the wiring, transfer equipment, circuits, grounding and controls that connect a generator to a building so selected loads can run safely during an outage. A generator alone does nothing without a code-compliant transfer switch, correctly sized conductors, and a tested connection to your panel.

Best fit: homes and businesses adding a standby or portable generator, upgrading or repairing a transfer switch, or planning which circuits stay powered during an outage.

Key takeaways

  • Every generator connection needs listed transfer equipment. Never backfeed through an outlet.
  • Size the system from a load calculation: running watts plus motor starting surge.
  • Automatic transfer switches start a standby generator for you; manual switches suit portable units.
  • Installations are normally permitted and inspected, and rules vary by city and state.
  • Test the system after installation and on a regular schedule so it works when needed.

Transfer Switches

What a transfer switch does and why it is required

A transfer switch moves your building between utility power and generator power and never lets both feed the same circuits. That protects line workers, the generator, and your electronics.

Manual transfer switches are operated by hand with a portable generator. Automatic transfer switches (ATS) sense an outage, signal the generator to start, transfer within seconds, and re-transfer when utility power returns. Service-rated units handle the whole building; circuit-based units power only the circuits you pick.

Sizing & Load Planning

How generator size is determined

  1. List what must stay on: refrigeration, heat or furnace blower, sump or well pump, lighting, internet, medical devices, cooking, A/C.
  2. Add running and starting loads: motors draw a surge on startup.
  3. Check service and panel capacity: the panel must handle the transfer equipment.
  4. Decide on load management: it lets a smaller generator serve large appliances in turn.
  5. Select generator and ATS ratings with margin for future loads.

Permits & Codes

Code and permit requirements for generators

Generator work is governed by the National Electrical Code as adopted locally, along with state and city amendments. Relevant NEC articles include Article 702 (optional standby systems), 700 (emergency), 701 (legally required standby), 445 (generators) and 705 (interconnected sources). Installations are normally permitted and inspected by the local authority having jurisdiction, and utilities may need notification.

Maintenance & Repair

Keeping backup power ready

A generator that has not been tested may not start when needed. Standby units typically run a short automatic exercise cycle and should be serviced on the manufacturer's schedule. We can verify transfer switch operation, check connections and breakers, and troubleshoot failures to transfer, start or carry load.

Safety First

Do not DIY hazardous generator connections

Never backfeed a building through an outlet or improvised cord. It can energize utility lines and injure line workers. Never run a generator indoors, in a garage, or near windows, doors or vents, because carbon monoxide can be fatal. Have transfer equipment professionally installed and tested.

See CPSC carbon monoxide safety and Ready.gov outage preparedness.

Glossary

Backup power terms explained

ATS (automatic transfer switch)
Detects utility loss, starts the generator and transfers the load without manual action.
Interlock
A device on the panel that prevents the main breaker and generator breaker from being on at the same time.
Load management
Controls that shed or sequence large loads so a generator is not overloaded.
Backfeed
Unsafe flow of generator power onto utility lines.
Standby generator
A permanently installed, fuel-piped generator that supplies backup power automatically.

FAQ

Generator & backup power questions answered

What is the difference between a portable and a standby generator?

A portable generator is started and connected by hand, usually to selected circuits through an inlet box and transfer equipment. A standby generator is permanently installed, runs on natural gas, propane or diesel, and starts automatically through an automatic transfer switch when utility power fails.

Do I need a transfer switch for a generator?

Yes. Transfer equipment isolates the building from the utility while the generator is running, which prevents dangerous backfeed to utility lines and protects the generator and your wiring. Plugging a generator into a wall outlet is not a substitute and is unsafe.

What is the difference between an automatic and a manual transfer switch?

A manual transfer switch is operated by a person after starting a portable generator. An automatic transfer switch (ATS) detects the outage, starts a standby generator, transfers the load, and returns to utility power when it is restored, with no one present.

Can a generator power my whole home?

Often, yes. It depends on generator capacity, the size of your electrical service, the type of transfer equipment, and which loads are included. Many homes use load management so a right-sized generator can run central air, ranges or EV chargers without being oversized.

What size generator do I need?

Size is based on the loads you want running at the same time, including the starting surge of motors such as air conditioners and well pumps. A load calculation, not a rule of thumb, determines the generator and transfer switch rating.

How much does generator installation cost?

Cost depends on generator size and fuel type, transfer switch type, distance from the panel and fuel source, trenching or conduit, panel capacity, permits, and site conditions. Trident Electric provides a scoped quote after reviewing your panel, loads and placement.

Do I need a permit to install a generator?

In most jurisdictions, yes. Generator and transfer switch installations are normally permitted and inspected by the local authority having jurisdiction, and some areas also require utility notification. Requirements vary by city and state.

Can I backfeed my house through a dryer outlet or a 'suicide cord'?

No. Backfeeding through an outlet can energize utility lines and endanger line workers and neighbors, and it can overload household wiring. Use a properly installed inlet box and listed transfer equipment instead.

How often should a generator and transfer switch be tested?

Standby generators typically run a short automatic exercise cycle weekly or biweekly and benefit from annual professional service following the manufacturer's schedule. Transfer switch operation should be verified at installation and periodically afterward.

Where can a standby generator be installed?

Outdoors, at a distance from windows, doors, vents and combustibles that satisfies the manufacturer's instructions and local code. Placement also depends on fuel access, noise, service access, and the length of the electrical run to the transfer switch.

Can you install commercial backup power?

Commercial electrical services can include generator and backup-power electrical work, depending on project scope. Life-safety, emergency and standby systems carry additional code, testing and documentation requirements that are planned up front.

Can a generator work with solar or battery storage?

It can, but interconnection must be designed so that generator, utility, solar and battery sources never feed each other unsafely. This is planned during load and equipment review before anything is installed.
