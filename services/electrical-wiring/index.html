Service Scope

What our electrical wiring & rewiring service covers

Wiring is the infrastructure behind outlets, lighting, appliances, panels and every other electrical load. The right solution depends on the existing system, equipment, circuit capacity, access, project goals and applicable electrical requirements.

New circuit wiring

Dedicated and general-purpose circuits for new outlets, lighting, appliances and equipment, sized to the load.

Whole-home rewiring

Replacing most or all branch-circuit wiring in older homes, often with a new panel and grounded, code-compliant circuits.

Partial rewiring

Replacing only unsafe, outdated or inaccessible circuits, such as a kitchen, an addition or a run of two-prong outlets.

240V appliance wiring

Ranges, dryers, EV chargers, heaters, hot tubs and shop equipment that need dedicated 240V circuits.

Garage, shop and detached buildings

Feeders, subpanels and branch circuits for garages, workshops, ADUs, sheds and outbuildings.

Remodel and addition wiring

New layouts for kitchens, baths, basements and additions, planned while walls are open.

Old wiring replacement

Evaluation and replacement of knob-and-tube, cloth-insulated, ungrounded or aluminum branch wiring.

Fault tracing

Finding and fixing damaged conductors, loose connections, overloaded circuits and dead outlets.

Wiring vs. Rewiring

Which option fits your project?

Many homes do not need a full rewire. A targeted repair or partial rewire can solve the problem at a lower cost, and an on-site evaluation shows which applies.

When to Call

10 signs your wiring may need repair or replacement

These are starting points, not a substitute for an electrical inspection or diagnosis.

Frequent breaker trips

Repeated trips, dead circuits or unexplained behavior can point to overloaded or faulty wiring.

Flickering or dimming lights

Lights that dim when appliances start can indicate loose connections or an overloaded circuit.

Warm, discolored or buzzing outlets

Heat, scorch marks or buzzing at a switch or outlet are safety concerns that need prompt attention.

Burning smell with no clear source

Turn off the affected circuit if it is safe to reach and call a qualified electrician.

Two-prong, ungrounded outlets

They lack an equipment ground and often signal older wiring that may need updating.

Cloth, rubber or knob-and-tube wiring

Visible in attics, basements and panels of older homes and worth a professional evaluation.

Not enough outlets

Extension cords and power strips are not a permanent substitute for added circuits.

Prior DIY or unpermitted work

Unknown splices, mismatched wire sizes and missing boxes are common hidden problems.

Renovation or addition planned

Opening walls is the lowest-cost time to correct or redesign wiring.

New high-load equipment

EV chargers, heat pumps, ranges and hot tubs may need new circuits and added panel capacity.

Older Wiring Types

Knob-and-tube, aluminum and ungrounded wiring

Homes built before modern standards may contain wiring that was acceptable when installed but does not suit today's loads. Knob-and-tube wiring, common in homes built before roughly the 1950s, has no ground conductor and can overheat when buried in insulation or overloaded. Aluminum branch-circuit wiring, used in many homes from about the mid-1960s to mid-1970s, needs connections rated for aluminum. Two-prong outlets usually mean the circuit has no equipment ground.

Findings like these do not automatically mean a whole-home rewire. Options range from adding grounding and protection devices, to replacing specific circuits, to a full rewire when conditions are widespread.

Modern protection that rewiring can add

  • Grounded circuits
  • GFCI protection in wet areas
  • AFCI protection on living-area circuits
  • Properly sized breakers and wire
  • Dedicated circuits for major appliances
  • Added panel capacity

Price depends on building size, the number of circuits and outlets, the age and type of existing wiring, how easy walls, attics and crawlspaces are to reach, panel capacity, permit and inspection requirements, and whether wall repair is needed. Safety First

Do not DIY hazardous electrical work

Do not cut into walls or splice unknown wiring based on assumptions. Hidden conductors can remain energized, and extension cords are not a permanent substitute for required wiring.

Smoke, sparks, burning smells, a hot or buzzing panel, or water near electrical equipment are safety concerns. If it is safe to reach, turn off the affected circuit at the breaker and call a qualified professional. In an emergency such as fire, call 911.

FAQ

Electrical wiring & rewiring FAQs

What is the difference between wiring and rewiring?

Wiring generally refers to installing or modifying electrical conductors and circuits. Rewiring usually means replacing or substantially changing existing wiring. A partial rewire replaces only the circuits or areas that are unsafe, outdated or being remodeled, while a whole-home rewire replaces most or all of a building's branch-circuit wiring.

How do I know if my house needs rewiring?

Common warning signs include frequently tripping breakers, flickering or dimming lights, warm or discolored outlets, burning smells, buzzing sounds, two-prong ungrounded outlets, visible cloth-insulated or knob-and-tube wiring, and prior DIY work. Only an on-site evaluation by a qualified electrician can confirm whether you need a partial rewire, a full rewire or a smaller repair.

Can you add wiring during a remodel?

Yes. Remodeling is a common time to add circuits, outlets, lighting, equipment wiring and other electrical changes while walls and ceilings are open and access is easier and usually less costly.

Do I need new wiring for a 240V appliance?

Many 240V appliances, such as ranges, dryers, EV chargers, heaters and hot tubs, require a dedicated, appropriately sized circuit with matching wire, breaker and receptacle. The exact requirements depend on the equipment nameplate and your electrical system's capacity.

Do I need a permit to rewire a house?

In most jurisdictions, new circuits and rewiring require an electrical permit and an inspection. Requirements depend on the work and the location. In Washington, the Department of Labor & Industries publishes the state's electrical rules, and a licensed contractor can explain what applies to your project.

How long does rewiring take?

A single new circuit may take hours to a day. A whole-home rewire commonly takes several days and can run longer for larger or harder-to-access buildings. Access, the age of the wiring, panel condition and inspection scheduling all affect the timeline.

Do I have to move out during a rewire?

Not always. Partial work is often done with the home occupied and power off only to the affected circuits. Whole-home rewiring can mean extended power interruptions, so some households choose to stay elsewhere for part of the project. Ask for a plan before work starts.

Will rewiring damage my walls?

Wiring in finished walls may require small access openings, and some jobs involve fishing wire through existing cavities to limit damage. Patching and painting are normally done by a drywall or painting contractor after the electrical work and inspection.

Is knob-and-tube or aluminum wiring dangerous?

Not automatically, but both are older wiring types that deserve professional evaluation. Knob-and-tube has no ground and can be a problem when covered by insulation or overloaded. Aluminum branch-circuit wiring from roughly the mid-1960s to mid-1970s needs properly rated connections. Options range from targeted repair to replacement.

What affects the cost of electrical wiring and rewiring?

Cost depends on the building's size, the number of circuits and outlets, wiring type and age, access to walls and attics, panel capacity, permit and inspection requirements, and whether wall repair is needed. Many jobs need an on-site assessment before a scope and price can be given.
