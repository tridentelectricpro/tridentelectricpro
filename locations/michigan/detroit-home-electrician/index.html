What electrical problems can an electrician help with in Detroit?

Common service categories include repairs and troubleshooting, wiring, panel upgrades, lighting, switches and outlets, installations, EV chargers, generators, surge protection, heaters, remodeling, additions, new homes, commercial and multifamily electrical work.

What information should I have ready when I call?

Describe what is happening, where it is happening, what equipment or circuit is involved, and whether the issue is ongoing or intermittent. For planned work, explain what you want installed or changed.

Can I repair an electrical panel, outlet or wiring myself?

Electrical work can be hazardous. DIY work is not recommended when you are not trained and equipped to safely diagnose or work on the system. Use an appropriately qualified electrical professional for electrical work.

Do you handle home remodeling and additions?

Can I call about a commercial or multifamily property?

Yes. Trident Electric has separate commercial and multifamily electrical service categories.

Is aluminum wiring in an older home a safety concern?

Aluminum wiring was common in homes built in the 1960s and 1970s, and it expands and loosens at connections more than copper does, which can cause overheating at outlets and switches. It does not always mean rewiring, since approved repair methods can make connections safe. An inspection can tell you whether your home has it and what to do.

What size generator do I need to keep essential circuits running?

Start by listing what must stay on during an outage, such as the refrigerator, some lights, medical equipment, and the furnace or well pump if you have one. The total draw of those essentials, plus the starting surge of motors, sets the generator size. We help you size it correctly so the essentials run without overloading the generator.

About Detroit

Electrical work in Detroit

Detroit's housing stock is among the oldest of any major American city, and that age shows up in its electrical systems. Many homes in the city were built before modern grounded wiring, and plenty still run on panels and service sizes that were sized for a different era of appliances. Knob-and-tube remnants, ungrounded two-prong outlets, and 60-amp services are common finds, and they are the reason panel upgrades and rewiring come up so often on Detroit jobs. Cold winters add their own strain: electric heat, space heaters, and furnace circuits work hard for months, and a panel that was already marginal in summer can start tripping once heating season arrives. The city's ongoing wave of renovation, from downtown rehabs to neighborhood restorations, usually means bringing a home's electrical system up to current standards before anything else is finished. If your Detroit home still has its original panel, fuses instead of breakers, or outlets that will not hold a plug, that is the place to start.

Local focus

Generators and backup power in Detroit

When the power goes out, the homes that cope best are usually the ones that planned for it, and that is the conversation we have with homeowners across Detroit. A standby generator can keep the heat on, the fridge cold, and the lights working through an outage, which matters most during storms and cold snaps. We help decide what size makes sense by looking at what the household wants to keep running, then we plan the placement, the fuel connection, and the transfer switch. During an installation visit in Michigan, we set the unit, wire it into the panel, and test the automatic switchover so the homeowner sees exactly what happens when the power drops.

Across Michigan

Electrical problems we hear about most across Michigan

Michigan's most common electrical issues come from its postwar housing stock, where 100-amp panels, ungrounded outlets, and wiring extended over decades of remodeling cannot keep up with modern loads. Long winters keep heating circuits under sustained demand, and lake-effect snow and ice storms cut power for hours. A cold garage can leave an EV charger underperforming, and humidity from the Great Lakes wears on outdoor equipment. Breakers that trip when the furnace and other large appliances run together are a familiar call.
